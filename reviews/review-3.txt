Vision

Homotopy Type theory (HoTT) is one of the most successful foundational frameworks in theoretical computer science, particularly well established in the UK and across Europe.

The proposal aims to extend HoTT to a more general framework, referred to as DTT. The central idea is to generalise “symmetry” into “function” as a primitive notion, such that symmetry becomes a derived concept.

First, I am not convinced that this extension is well justified. In
HoTT, equality is already a rich structure, and its symmetric nature
provides an inherently structured and reversible representation,
contributing to elegant proofs. Symmetry also offers natural
connections to geometry, topology, and category theory. The proposal
does not clearly explain why DTT is needed or why HoTT is
insufficient. In particular, no convincing case studies or motivating
examples are provided. It is plausible that adopting “function as a
primitive” may complicate the metatheory, sacrificing the elegance and
tractability of HoTT without delivering clear benefits.

Section 1.3 (“Quality, Impact and Timeliness”) does not resolve these
concerns. HoTT already has significant impact in areas such as formal
verification and category theory. The proposed framework appears
closer to an incremental extension (HoTT+) rather than a fundamentally
new direction. Furthermore, the proposal’s vision seems targeted
primarily at specialists in HoTT, rather than a broader ICT audience,
making its wider significance unclear. The title of this project
"Bridging category theory and HoTT" is also unclear since HoTT has
already a link to category theory.

While the project may contribute to world-leading research within
HoTT, its impact beyond academia—particularly on society, the economy,
or the environment—is limited. The work remains within  foundational developments.

Approach

The proposed approach follows a standard pattern for theoretically driven research: developing a new theory along a prototype implementation (in Agda or Narya).

Two main concerns are:

Structures and Targets

The project involves three leading theoretical computer scientists,
each committing 20% of their time, supervising a single PDRA. 
The division of responsibilities among the investigators is not
clearly articulated, and the workplan does not sufficiently clarify
individual roles. Vague expressions such as “a bit later,”
“prototypical implementation,” and “tweak” reduce a technical precision of the proposal and make it difficult to assess feasibility.

In Work Package 7 (WP7), applications to mathematics and physics are mentioned, but no concrete examples are provided to demonstrate why DTT is necessary—for instance, in modelling higher quantum computation, which is already addressed in HoTT.

WP7 also proposes developing “suitable use cases” through collaboration with theoretical physicists, yet no specific collaborators are identified. The proposal references “non-invertible symmetries” in high-energy physics but does not provide citations.

Risk mitigation strategies are similarly vague. For example, the statement that “partial success would make significant progress” is not defined (operationally), leaving uncertainty about evaluation criteria.

Implementation

Developing a prototype in Agda is consistent with the project’s category-theoretic and semantic foundations. However, for applications such as quantum verification, researchers typically rely on mature libraries and infrastructures (e.g., QWIRE, VOQC, SQIR in Rocq). Using Agda may require building substantial infrastructure from scratch.

Quantum computing involves complex algebraic structures (matrices, complex numbers, linear algebra), which are known to be challenging to formalise efficiently. Agda does not currently offer a mature or easily usable linear algebra ecosystem, and encoding such structures can become verbose and technically demanding. The proposal does not address how these challenges will be managed, nor does it justify the choice of tools.

Narya has even deeper concerns regarding maturity and stability, as well as limited potential impact.

Applicant and Team Capability

The investigators are highly distinguished researchers in the field. Altenkirch is a leading figure in type theory, and Kraus and Buchholtz are also excellent and active contributors. Their collaboration within the same institution (Nottingham) has been already well established (see below); and the project proposal consists of letters from their leading international collaborators.

However, the necessity of the PDRA position is not clearly justified. Based on the work packages, it appears that the investigators, together with existing collaborators and PhD students, could achieve most of project goals without additional staffing.

Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)

Kraus currently holds multiple major fellowships (University Fellowship, ERC, Royal Society Fellowship). The proposal does not clearly explain how this project is distinct from or complementary to these existing efforts.

Although the RRI section highlights past industrial impact of HoTT, there is no supporting evidence of current industry engagement (e.g., letters of support). The proposal therefore appears primarily academic in scope.

A minor comment: I cannot find a description of Buchholtz's activities in Module 2.

Resources

The justification of resources is generally well described. However,
allocating 20% effort from each of three investigators within the same
department is not convincingly justified. The proposal stays 
within an internal collaboration (with many academia in abroad) rather than a necessity for substantial external funding.

The investigators already supervise a significant number of
students and researchers (7 PDRAs and 13 PhD students collectively), raising the question of why additional funding—60% of combined PI time—is required.

Summary

Overall, the proposal raises several unsolvable concerns. It remains
unclear why DTT is necessary, how it improves upon HoTT, and how it
will achieve the goals outlined in WP7 beyond existing approaches. The
choice of implementation tools is insufficiently justified,
particularly in relation to quantum computing applications.

No concrete or compelling use cases are provided to demonstrate the
need for DTT. While the investigators are likely to produce
high-quality academic papers, the broader impact on society and the
economy are highly limited. Despite references to industrial relevance
in RRI, no supporting evidence from industry partners is included.

Given a strong international collaboration already in place, the proposal might be more suitable for a UKRI Overseas Travel Grant (OTG), which would support further development of HoTT through collaboration rather than a large research grant.
